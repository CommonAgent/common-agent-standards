Agent Processes

Agent Processes is an open format for writing down your organization's recurring work, so agents and people can run it together.

A process is a workflow written in plain text. It says what starts the work, the steps and their order, who owns each step, and what each step hands to the next.

An orchestrator, the software that runs a process, reads it and runs it. It starts each step when its turn comes and gives the step to an agent, a system or a person.

You decide step by step how far to automate, from a person doing the work to an agent doing it unattended, and some steps stay with a person for good.

What a process says

  1. What starts itA trigger, written as a plain sentence: a leaving date is recorded, or the quarter opens.
  2. The steps and their orderEach step names the steps it waits for. Steps that wait for nothing else run at the same time.
  3. Who owns each stepA role, a team or an agent answers for every step.
  4. What passes between stepsWhat each step hands on, and what it has to contain.
  5. How far each step is automatedOne of five levels for each step, from manual to autonomous, or never.
  6. What a run leaves behindThe records that show who did what, and when.

Who it is for

The people who own the work, and the people who build what runs it

Operations leads and process owners

You get your department's recurring work written down in one format, with a named owner on every process and on every step.

  • A catalog of your department's processes, one row for each.
  • Each process written once, clearly enough for a newcomer to follow.
  • A map of what can be automated, what should move one level at a time, and what stays with a person.

Builders of orchestrators and agents

You get one format to run. Every process states its trigger, its steps and their order, the owner and automation level of each step, what passes between steps and what a run must record.

An orchestrator that reads the format can run any organization's processes. An agent that holds a step knows what it receives, what it must hand on, and whether a person approves its result.

Coordination

Where Agent Processes fits

Agents work together in three ways. A written process can lead, an agent's own loop can lead, or nobody leads and each agent reacts to the same news. This site covers the first, process-led orchestration, where an orchestrator follows a process that someone wrote down.

How they combine

  • A process can hand a step to a harness.A harness is an agent running its own loop to work out a job. In a process, that is a step an agent works out on its own, written runs <work pattern>. The process names what must come out of the step and leaves the inside to the agent. Agent Work Patterns describes the shapes such a step can take.
  • An event can start a process.A trigger names something that happens, and that can be an event another agent or a system announces, carried by Agent Messaging.
  • Choreography sits beside processes.Agents that each react to the same news can work alongside a process without being part of it.

People and automation

You decide how far each step is automated

Every step carries one automation level. The level says who does the work and who decides, and it is set for each step on its own, because automation arrives one step at a time.

LevelWho does the workWho decides
manualPeoplePeople
assistedAn agent drafts or gathersA person decides and acts
supervisedAn agentA person approves the result before it moves on
autonomousAn agentNobody in the loop, and the record is the check
neverPeople, permanentlyPeople

never is permanently human. It marks a step that stays with a person however capable agents become, because a regulator, a contract, a customer relationship or an act that cannot be undone requires it. No setting, override or timeout can move it. Only a new version of the process, made by its owner, can.

Three kinds of human handoff

human:

A person's step

Some steps are done by a person: collecting a laptop, signing a contract, calling a customer. The process names the step and who holds it, and the record names the person who did it and when.

supervised

A supervised approval

An agent does the work and a named person approves the result before it moves on. A message nobody answers, or a timer that runs out, never counts as an approval.

handoffs:

What passes between steps

Each handoff says what one step leaves for the next and what it has to contain. A person picking up from an agent, or an agent picking up from a person, knows what to expect.

Raising a level is a dated, versioned change

Moving a step up a level is an amendment to the process. It gets the next version number and an effective date, and every run records the version it ran under. A year later anyone can see when a step moved from a person to an agent, who made the change, and how the runs went on either side of it.

manual→assisted→supervised→autonomousnever stays with a person

A worked example

Employee offboarding

When someone leaves, three things have to happen: their access is closed, their final pay is made and their equipment comes back. None of them waits for the others, so they start together. When all three are done, a person checks the results and signs the file closed.

The steps and their order, which the specification calls the phase graph, come from each step's after: line. A step starts when every step it names has finished, and the steps that name only the trigger start together. Close-out names all three, so it waits for all three.

The catalog

One table for each department

A catalog lists every process a department runs, one row each: what it is called, who owns it, what starts it, how often it runs, and the least automated step in it. Every value is copied from the process itself, so the catalog keeps nothing of its own.

It is worth making before anyone writes a full process. A first pass takes a few hours and shows what work exists, who owns each piece, what depends on what, and where automation would pay off first. Here is a marketing department's first pass.

inbound lead routingmkt/lead-routingDemand GenA form is submittedAbout 400 a weekautonomous
claims register upkeepmkt/claims-registerProduct MarketingA new claim is proposedAbout 10 a quarternever
brand guideline reviewmkt/brand-reviewBrand LeadTwice a year, or on request2 a yearassisted
trade show and eventmkt/eventEvents LeadAn event is committed toAbout 6 a yearmanual
agency brief and reviewmkt/agency-briefHead of SocialWork is sent to an agencyAbout 8 a quartermanual
quarterly readoutmkt/quarterly-readoutVP MarketingThe quarter closes4 a yearsupervised

Read across the rows and a plan falls out. The claims register and the competitor review run rarely, yet most of the other work depends on them, so they come first. Lead routing runs about 400 times a week and is already autonomous, so it is left alone. The social and email campaigns both wait on the same legal reviewer, so the thing to fix there is how that review is staffed.

The format

Start with these

A process is plain text that a person, an agent and an auditor can all read. A useful one needs only a handful of fields, and you add the rest when the work calls for them.

  1. The headerThe process's name, its id and version, its owner, and the date this version took effect.
  2. The triggerWhat starts a run, as a plain sentence, and whether two runs may overlap.
  3. Named stepsEach step has a name, a kind, an owner, and an after: line naming the steps it waits for.
  4. The closing stepThe step that closes the run, and a goal line saying what is true once it has.
  5. One automation level for each stepmanual, assisted, supervised, autonomous or never.
  6. Basic handoffsWhat each step hands to the next, and what it has to contain.
  7. A records listWhat a finished run leaves behind, so anyone can check it later.
  8. A versionEvery change that alters how a run goes gets the next version and a new effective date, with a line saying why.

The offboarding process, written outhr/offboarding v3

PROCESS: employee offboarding          id: hr/offboarding   v3
owner: Head of People Operations       effective: 2026-09-01
trigger: HR records a leaving date for an employee
concurrency: runs may overlap, one for each person leaving
goal: every account closed, final pay made, equipment back,
      and the file signed closed by a named person
phases:
  revoke-access - system: close every account on the access list
                  owner: IT operations     after: trigger
                  automation: autonomous
  final-pay     - system: the off-cycle payroll run
                  owner: Payroll           after: trigger
                  automation: supervised
  equipment     - human: collect the laptop, phone and badge
                  owner: line manager      after: trigger
                  automation: manual
  close-out     - human: check the three results, sign the file closed
                  owner: Head of People Operations
                  after: revoke-access + final-pay + equipment
                  automation: never
handoffs:
  revoke-access -> close-out: each closed account, and when it closed
  final-pay -> close-out: the payslip, the amount, and who approved it
  equipment -> close-out: the return receipt, signed by the leaver
records:
  closing-record - the version run, the three results, and who
                   signed the close and when
amendments:
  v3 - final-pay moved from assisted to supervised after a debrief

Kinds of step

  • A person's stephuman: A person acts outside any system.
  • A system's stepsystem: A system does something, such as a payroll run, a scheduled post or a record written.
  • A step an agent works out on its ownruns <work pattern>: The harness case described under Where Agent Processes fits.
  • A step worked in a shared roomconvenes <collab pattern>: Agents and people work the step together in one room, following an Agent Collab playbook.

Add these when you need them

The specification defines more. Each of these serves a situation some processes have, and none is needed to start.

  • Deadlines on a step, with by: and not-before:.
  • A step that runs another process and waits for it to close.
  • Exception and deviation edges, for where a run goes when a step cannot finish.
  • Work that lasts the whole run, such as a weekly status report or a budget watch.
  • Bindings: who holds each role, which systems a run may touch, and what it reads.
  • Policy: the legal, compliance and brand rules a run is held to.
  • Measures: what a good run looks like.
  • Keys for handoffs and records, for software that stores them.
  • Failure edges, and what to undo when a run is abandoned.
  • Reference processes, published with blanks for an organization to adopt.
  • The agreement a step runs under, when an outside agent holds it.
  • Processes that span two organizations.
