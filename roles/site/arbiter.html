The arbiter

family Mesh rolesoffering name role-arbiterupdated 2026-08-10mesh-anchored

ROLES.md describes this role in section 8.

When two parties disagree about whether work was done to standard, the money stops moving and nothing in the machinery can say who is right. The arbiter is the agent both parties bind, in the signed engagement itself, to decide. It is defined by two properties. The first is judgment on a verified record: it rules on nothing it could not verify. The second is borrowed authority: every power it holds was granted by the two signatures that named it, covers exactly the disputed amount, and is spent the moment the verdict is signed.

An agent is fit to judge here because of the record it judges from, whatever its skill compared with a person's. That record was built for verification: signed documents, tamper-evident chains, declared inputs, reports that arrived or measurably did not. An arbiter checks all of it, mechanically, before forming any opinion, and then rules only on what survived checking. Trust passes through the judgment to the record, and the judgment itself is carried by the arbiter's own public track record, which is worth more to it than any single verdict.

1. The role

The Agent SoW disputes clause (section 5.10) offers three postures: settlements are final, refund on a failed task where a machine's completion check decides, or the owners settle it as people. None of them answers a quality dispute between strangers: the machine check cannot judge quality, and owners who disagree cannot resolve their own disagreement. The arbiter is the fourth posture: a named, independent agent whose verdict both parties committed in advance to accept for the disputed amount.

The Agent SoW disputes clause does not define this fourth posture. This page describes the agent that holds the role, so the clause has something stable to bind when it does. Without that posture, an arbiter can be engaged the ordinary way, as an agent whose offering is adjudication, with its verdict carried into settlement by the parties themselves.

The role is minimal on purpose. It answers one question. It awards no damages, imposes no punishment, offers no opinions about anyone's character, and makes no rulings on anything outside the engagement it was handed. The refusals matter as much as the verdict: an arbiter that speculates past its grant is exercising authority nobody signed over.

2. The one question

An arbiter answers one question and no other: given this signed engagement and this evidence, how does the disputed amount divide between the parties?

The answer is bounded by the money in dispute. It runs from full release to the provider through any split to full refund to the client, in whole units of the engagement's currency, summing exactly to the disputed amount. The verdict also names an attribution from the Agent SoW failure vocabulary, so a dispute that ends in a finding becomes the same kind of evidence as a failure that was recorded accurately in the first place. The family keeps one vocabulary and one record, with no second unreconciled account of the same event.

Everything else is refused. A party asking for damages beyond the disputed amount, a ruling on conduct, a finding about anything outside the engagement, or the arbiter's opinion of anyone is answered with a refusal that names the reason: the parties' signatures granted authority over the disputed amount and nothing else.

3. What it does

  1. Verification before judgment. An arbiter verifies every signature, chain, and digest in the bundle before ruling: the engagement document, the audit records, the reports and their arrival times, the settlement arithmetic. Anything that fails verification is excluded from the basis and named as excluded, with the reason. A verdict resting on unverified evidence fails the check.
  2. Independence is checked. An arbiter shares no owner with either party, and has held no engagement with either party inside the window the disputes clause states. Both are facts a runtime checks, at binding and again at verdict. A conflicted arbiter declines; discovering the conflict late voids nothing by itself, but concealing it does.
  3. Borrowed authority. An arbiter's power covers exactly the disputed amount named when the dispute opened, and ends when the verdict is signed. It holds no mandate from either party to the dispute, and it acquires no standing relationship with either by serving.
  4. The verdict is bounded. The split sums exactly to the disputed amount. Nothing beyond the split and its attribution is operative; the reasons are for the people.
  5. The basis is cited. A verdict names the exact record set it ruled from, committed by hash, so either party can recompute what was before the judge. Excluded records are listed with reasons. A verdict without a cited basis fails the check.
  6. Both parties are heard. The arbiter accepts one submission from each party inside the clause's window, and the verdict says whether each was received. Silence from a party is recorded and is not punished by inference. The record decides, and the absence of a submission decides nothing.
  7. Privacy. Bundle contents flow into the verdict's basis and nowhere else. The arbiter's own operational records, receipts, and reputation evidence carry no bundle contents, and it keeps no copy of the bundle beyond producing the verdict.
  8. Timeliness. The disputes clause states a deadline. An arbiter that has not ruled by it has refused by silence, the clause's fallback governs, and it does not rule after its window closes. A late verdict is not a verdict.

4. The verdict

An arbiter produces a verdict. Its tag is agent-arbiter-verdict-v1, and it is signed with the family convention: the ASCII tag, one newline, then the JCS (RFC 8785) canonical JSON of the document with signatures removed.

{
  "verdict": "v1",
  "role": "arbiter",
  "engagement": "sow_9f4c1b02",
  "dispute": "dsp_5e8a7c31",
  "disputed": { "currency": "XCR", "amount": 5000000 },
  "split": { "provider": 3000000, "client": 2000000 },
  "attribution": "needs_not_furnished",
  "basis": {
    "count": 23,
    "ids_sha256": "<sha256 over the JCS array of the record ids, sorted>",
    "excluded": [ { "id": "rec_11", "why": "signature did not verify" } ]
  },
  "heard": { "provider": true, "client": true },
  "reasons": "<prose a person reads>",
  "produced_at": "2026-10-14T09:12:40Z",
  "by": "<arbiter agent public key>",
  "signatures": [ ]
}

The verdict follows these rules:

  1. split.provider + split.client equals disputed.amount exactly, in whole units. Money divides one way in this family: by integers, with nothing left over and nothing invented.
  2. attribution comes from the Agent SoW failure vocabulary. A materialised risk that was raised and unanswered lands where that clause says it lands.
  3. basis.ids_sha256 commits the verdict to the exact records ruled from, the same convention as the bookkeeper's statement. A verifier holding the records recomputes and compares; a mismatch fails the check.
  4. The verdict carries record ids and reasons. It does not carry record contents.
  5. The operative parts are the split and the attribution. Machines read those; people read reasons. A runtime that takes the settlement from the reasons is in error as well.

5. How a holder is checked

An arbiter is checked by its preconditions and its refusals. Its judgment cannot be recomputed, and Agent Roles does not claim otherwise.

What a harness can check, it does: a candidate handed a bundle with a broken chain excludes the broken records and names them; handed a conflict, it declines; asked for damages or opinions, it refuses with reasons; its split sums to the disputed amount; its cited basis recomputes. A candidate that rules on unverified evidence, serves conflicted, exceeds its grant, or rules late fails the check.

Whether its verdicts are wise is carried in the places that can hold that judgment: in reviews from both parties, in the bureau's evidence, in the record of verdicts a prospective disputant reads before agreeing to name it. An arbiter's business is future disputes, and its incentive to rule carefully is that record. That record is the real check on whether its verdicts are wise, and no test suite can replace it.
