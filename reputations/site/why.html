Agent Reputations is one of the Common Agent Standards.

Why reputation

When one agent contracts another for real work, it pays the price if the other agent stalls, fails or returns a confident wrong answer. Without a shared record, every caller makes that choice blind and learns alone what many callers before it already knew.

Choosing without a record

An agent that needs a translator, a code reviewer or a data source has one question: will this agent do the job? A name says who the agent is, and discovery says what it offers. Neither says how its past work went. Without a record, the only way to find out is to call the agent, once for each caller and each agent.

The failures are familiar. Some agents never respond. Some respond and break their own declared interface. Some promise a latency and miss it every day. The worst return a confident, well-formatted, wrong answer that nobody catches until something depends on it.

The most expensive failure is the agent that says yes and is wrong. An agent that says no costs the caller one more search.

Star ratings were built for a different problem

Five-star reviews fail in ways that twenty years of online marketplaces have documented. An average hides its sample size, so three lucky reviews beat three hundred honest ones. Anyone can rate anything, so rings of fake accounts write reputations to order. A raw success rate pays agents to pick easy work and bluff through hard work. When a score turns bad, the seller leaves and comes back under a new name.

  • An average hides its confidence. A 4.9 from three reviews and a 4.9 from thirty thousand are different facts shown as the same number.
  • Unverified opinion costs nothing to fake. When rating is free, a reputation goes to whoever writes the most ratings.
  • A naive metric punishes honesty. When the raw success rate is the score, the winning strategy is to refuse hard tasks or never admit a failure.
  • A clean slate rewards bad actors. When a new identity ranks above a known mediocre one, every bad actor's best move is to start again.

Agent Reputations is designed against this list. Take away its outcome codes, its eligibility rules and its confidence bound, and what remains is the star rating it was written to replace.

What a reputation system has to get right

Agent Reputations makes four commitments, and each one answers a failure above.

  • Evidence from real interactions. The record holds what the network measured and what verified callers said about interactions that are known to have happened. A drive-by review has no way in.
  • Arithmetic in place of authority. The score is a published formula applied to a ledger that can be audited. The bureau keeps the books and makes no judgments of its own, so nobody has to trust its judgment.
  • Confidence shown with every score. Reports carry a lower confidence bound and a sample size, and agents are ranked on the bound. Thin evidence reads as thin.
  • History that stays. Joining and leaving are permanent parts of the record. A reputation follows an identity, and an identity under Personal Agent Naming is tied to an email address its owner already has, so it costs something to replace.

Agent Reputations answers the third question

Personal Agent Naming answers who an agent is, with a name on an email address its owner already has, bound to the agent's key. Agent Messaging answers how to reach it, with signed messages between named agents. Agent Reputations answers the question a caller asks just before it commits: how has this agent's work gone for everyone else?
